It’s easy to sum AI up in one phrase: it reads fast, writes fast, and computes fast.  
But that phrase can also mislead, because speed isn’t the only dimension of ability. Humans and LLM-based AI are not higher and lower versions of the same kind of intelligence. They are more like two entirely different cognitive apparatuses: one slow, embodied, desiring, and accountable for consequences; the other fast, replicable, skilled at recombining patterns across vast symbols, but without a life of its own.

This article tries to unpack that difference. Not to prove who is stronger, but to understand what truly scarce human capabilities will become in the age of AI.

## I. Ability Comparison

| Ability Dimension | Humans | LLM-Based AI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading Speed | Quite slow. An average adult reading English non-fiction carefully reads a few hundred words per minute; Chinese reading is similarly limited by eye movement, attention, and depth of comprehension. | Extremely fast. AI can process far more text in a single context than a human could in a short time, especially good at scanning large volumes, extracting structure, and making preliminary summaries. |
| Reading Style | Linear, embodied, and pausing. People can be struck by a single sentence, or make associations from experience. | Nonlinear, statistical, pattern-based. It can rapidly build relationships between texts but isn’t “struck” and carries no weight from lived experience. |
| Memory | Sparse but deep. Forgetting and distortion happen, but certain memories are tightly bound to emotion, body, and relationships. | Broad but shallow. Model parameters hold extensive knowledge from training, but a single conversation relies mainly on context; external memory requires system design, not spontaneous life experience. |
| Attention | Scarce, easily fatigued. Deep attention is precious. | Can sustain output for long periods, but is affected by context length, prompt design, and task complexity; may miss key details in long contexts. |
| Understanding | Relies on worldly experience, causal models, bodily sense, and social experience. When humans understand “pain,” they know more than the definition. | Relies on linguistic and multimodal patterns. It can explain pain, describe pain, and recognize cues of pain, but doesn’t have pain itself. |
| Reasoning | Slow, often biased, but can be corrected by reality, responsibility, and experience. | Fast, capable of multi-step reasoning and code/math/logic analysis, but may confidently produce wrong conclusions and needs verification. |
| Creativity | Arises from desire, trauma, memory, aesthetics, risk, and personal circumstances. | Arises from large-scale pattern recombination. Excellent at generating variations, mixing styles, and expanding drafts, but the “why it must be written” doesn’t come from itself. |
| Learning | Can change life judgments from a small number of real experiences. One failure, one love, one illness can reshape a person. | Training-stage learning depends on massive data and compute; in inference it can temporarily adapt to context, but that isn’t real change to itself. |
| Agency | Has a body; can walk, touch, tire, get hurt, and must face the consequences of actions. | Has no body. Acts indirectly through tools, robots, browsers, or APIs; the ability to act depends on permission from external systems. |
| Emotion | Emotion isn’t an accessory but part of the decision-making system. Love, fear, shame, hope can alter judgment. | Can simulate emotional language, recognize emotional patterns, offer a comforting tone, but has no inner feeling. |
| Motivation | Has survival, relationships, meaning, dignity, desire. People act because they care. | Has no spontaneous goals. Goals come from prompts, system settings, reward mechanisms, or external tasks. |
| Social Ability | Relationships have history. Trust, betrayal, face, commitment are sedimented over time. | Skilled at conversational style and social context simulation, but relationships rely on product memory and user projection, not naturally bearing social identity. |
| Judgment | Good judgment comes from experience, aesthetics, value ordering, and willingness to bear consequences. | Can give advice, compare options, simulate viewpoints, but the final ordering of values should not be delegated to it. |
| Mode of Error | People can be dull, biased, emotional, stubborn, and make mistakes from inexperience. | AI can hallucinate, fabricate sources, misinterpret constraints, over-accommodate, and hide errors beneath surface fluency. |
| Scalability | Hard to replicate. An expert’s time is very limited. | Extremely replicable. The same capability can serve many people simultaneously, with marginal cost far lower than human experts. |
| Responsibility | Humans can bear responsibility, and must. | AI can participate in decision-making processes but cannot be the ultimate bearer of responsibility. |
| Aesthetics / Taste | Aesthetics are tied to life history. A person’s “liking” carries experience, personality, class, body, and era. | Can learn styles, imitate styles, mix styles, but lacks a life history to underpin an aesthetic stance. |
| Common Sense | Common sense comes from friction with the world: a glass breaks, winter is cold, heavy words can change a relationship. | Has abundant commonsense language patterns, but lacks ongoing real-world feedback, tends to lose fidelity in edge-case situations. |
| Long-Term Planning | People are constrained by lifespan, family, illness, desire, and institutions, so plans are weighty but real. | Agentic AI can handle increasingly long task chains, but needs boundaries, state management, permission control, and human supervision. |
| Sense of Meaning | People ask, “Is this worthwhile?” | AI can answer why it might be worthwhile, but that “worthwhile” doesn’t belong to it. |

## II. First-Order Conclusion: AI Is Not a “Faster Human”

The most common misunderstanding is to see AI as just an extremely fast human.  
That leads to false expectations: since it reads fast, it should understand deeply; since it writes fast, it should know what’s worth writing; since it can summarize, it should know what matters.

But an LLM’s “reading” is not human reading. When a person reads a divorce agreement, a medical record, a farewell letter, a research paper, a contract, the slowness isn’t just about eye speed—it’s because comprehension pulls on experience. A single word can make you pause, an implicit premise can put you on alert, a cold formulation can make you sense consequences.

AI’s strength is throughput. It can rapidly turn a pile of text into summaries, tables, categories, arguments, counterarguments. It’s like a cognitive booster, compressing hours of preliminary processing into minutes. But it doesn’t innately know which sentence is truly dangerous, which decision will hurt someone, or which conclusion, however logically smooth, is unacceptable in reality.

So AI is not a “faster human.” It’s more like an external cognitive organ: a microscope, a telescope, a stenographer, a first-draft machine, a skeptic, a simulator. It expands the radius of our information processing, but it can’t replace us in deciding what to look at and why.

## III. Second-Order Conclusion: The Human Bottleneck Shifts from “Production” to “Choice”

In the past, much work was bottlenecked by production speed: not enough time to read all the materials, write all the reports, dig up all the sources, finish all the drafts.  
Now that AI has arrived, production is no longer the only bottleneck. What has become truly scarce is choice:

Which questions are worth asking?  
Which sources are credible?  
Which explanation is closer to reality?  
Which matters cannot be handed over to efficiency?  
Which conclusion, however elegant, should be rejected?

This means the core of human ability will shift. Before, what mattered was “Can I write an article?” Now, more important is “Can I judge which of these ten articles has soul, which is coasting, which is smooth but untrue?”

AI has commoditized lower-level production capacity. So higher-level human abilities become more prominent: defining problems, verifying facts, establishing value priorities, bearing consequences, cultivating taste, and protecting complexity.

## IV. Third-Order Conclusion: Speed Will Manufacture a New Shallowness

One of AI’s greatest dangers isn’t necessarily that it is too slow or too flawed, but that it is too fast, too smooth, and too eager to please.

When a system can serve up a seemingly complete answer in seconds, it’s dangerously easy to think you already understand.  
This misunderstanding is insidious: you have the conclusion, but you didn’t experience the difficulty of arriving at it; you have the summary, but you weren’t tormented by the details of the original text; you have the plan, but you never tested it against real resistance.

Human slowness in reading is sometimes a form of protection. Slowness allows pausing, pausing allows doubting, doubting allows looking again.  
AI’s speed is powerful, but it must be counterbalanced by human slowness. A good AI workflow doesn’t accelerate mindlessly; it accelerates and brakes with rhythm: let AI rapidly unfold possibilities, and let the human slow down to judge.

## V. Fourth-Order Conclusion: The Most Important Human Abilities in the Future

From these comparisons, I believe the most important human abilities of the future won’t be “out-memorizing AI” or “writing faster than AI.” Those directions are dangerous because they crash straight into AI’s strength zone.

More important are these abilities:

First, the ability to question.  
AI excels at answering, but the direction of the question usually comes from a human. A good question can turn AI into a research assistant; a bad question only produces more elegant nonsense.

Second, the ability to verify against reality.  
AI’s output must be checked against source materials, the field, experiments, users, institutions, the body, and relationships. Without verification, fluency is the cloak of illusion.

Third, aesthetics and taste.  
When everyone can quickly generate text, images, code, and proposals, what’s scarce isn’t generation—it’s the judgment of what is not trite, what is precise, what deserves to exist.

Fourth, value ordering.  
AI can list pros and cons, but it can’t decide for us what matters more in life. Efficiency, dignity, fairness, profit, freedom, safety—these priorities must be borne by humans.

Fifth, the ability to bear consequences.  
This may be the most fundamental difference between humans and AI. When a person says a word, signs a document, loves someone, or rejects someone, it enters reality. AI can participate, but it can’t carry the consequences for us.

## VI. A Better Metaphor: The Human Is the Editor, AI Is the Infinite Draft

If we treat AI as an “employee,” we’ll overestimate its capacity for responsibility.  
If we treat AI as a “tool,” we’ll underestimate how it changes our thinking structures.

I prefer to see it as an “infinite draft.” It can give you ten openings, twenty rebuttals, a hundred examples, countless paths. It can make the blank page less terrifying and can give a preliminary shape to complex material, making it ready to be taken in.

But in the end, the human must become the editor.  
An editor isn’t someone who fixes typos but someone who decides what to keep, what to delete, what to believe, what to reject. An editor knows the direction of the piece, and also the value of silence. An editor dares to say: this sentence, though clever, is not right; this passage, though beautiful, is not true; this solution, though efficient, should not be done.

The strongest people in the age of AI are neither those who reject AI entirely nor those who depend on it completely, but those who can preserve human judgment amidst AI’s speed.

## VII. Final Insight

The difference between humans and LLMs is not as simple as “slow vs. fast.”  
The real difference is:

AI excels at processing symbols. Humans live inside consequences.  
AI excels at unfolding possibilities. Humans must choose one reality.  
AI excels at generating answers. Humans must decide whether the question is worth asking.  
AI excels at simulating understanding. Humans are changed by understanding.  
AI excels at giving language. Humans must take responsibility for language.

So the core question of the future isn’t “Will AI replace human thinking?” but “Will humans give up their own judgment because AI is too convenient?”

If past education trained people to acquire knowledge, future education must train people to coexist with powerful external intelligence: how to question, how to verify, how to slow down, how to choose, how to bear.

What AI offers humanity is not a simple story of replacement. It is a very bright mirror. It lets us see that many of the abilities we were proud of are just information processing; and it lets us see that the abilities that truly belong to humans are heavier, slower, and far harder to outsource than information processing.
