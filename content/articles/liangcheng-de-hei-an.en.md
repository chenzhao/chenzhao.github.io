I always thought I lived by order.
Not by a person, but by order. Order spares you from having to prove yourself; you just remember to stand in your designated place, attend meetings on time, sign on time, smile on time.

To me, Liangcheng was never a city, but the shape of a system: the streets were clauses, the elevators were procedures, the office was a lamp slowly turning on the ceiling. Every time I stood by the conference-room window and watched the row of old willows across the way being bent sideways by the north wind, I gave myself a score in my mind: qualified again today—no transgression, no delay, no loss of composure. Those scores were like a competition; no one ever announced a winner, yet everyone knew who was at the bottom.

When I was young, I was proud. Not because I was exceptionally capable, but because my paperwork was the neatest, my briefings the calmest. If a superior said, “You can take this forward,” I would add three pages of appendices that very night. If a subordinate said, “It’s a bit tricky,” I would instantly turn it into an actionable formulation.
From the moment I first sat down at my desk in the bureau, I saw myself as someone “usable”: usable by meeting rooms, usable by documents, usable by those who needed someone to speak the right words for them—to speak properly, to smooth away the contradictions.

Of course I took pleasure in using myself that way, too. Standing up in meetings, articulating clearly; going downstairs and seeing colleagues nod; coming home and hearing my wife say, “You’re so tired again today—finally got through it.”

Back then I didn’t yet know that a person often calls himself “hardworking” simply because he’s afraid of being forced to admit that he is nothing more than a replaceable cog.

The real change didn’t begin with an event; it began inside language.
The first time I noticed was after a “major advancement meeting.” The pain appeared first in my lower back—a stifling, prickling ache. At first, as usual, I attributed it simply to late nights and damp weather. That was my greatest skill: giving every nameless thing a professional name.

So I said: stomach-ache.
The doctor said: keep observing.
I said: too much work.
I told myself: this is only temporary.

The word “temporary” is very useful: it allows you to lie down for a while without admitting that you’re falling. Day after day, I left my signature on papers just as before. The signature was like a talisman, telling the people above, “I’m still here.”

But the body fights back exactly where you think you can discount it to the system. That night, in the car home, I suddenly doubled over like a student who hadn’t done his homework, fingers clutching the handrail. The driver asked if I was cold.
I said no.
In fact, for the first time I heard a sound inside my belly, a very soft, very heavy splintering: if I can’t get up tomorrow, can your position stand up for you?

I told no one. Not until a colleague urged me to go to the hospital did I finally admit, grudgingly, that it wasn’t some minor ailment a puff of wind could blow away.

After I was hospitalized, the first few days were shame.
Shame at being treated as an individual, not “Section Chief Zhao.” Shame when someone taking my blood pressure seemed visibly impatient. Shame when a nurse wiped my forehead with a movement more powerful than years of my work directives.

What troubled me most wasn’t the pain.
It was that every visitor came with a different version of “encouragement”:
The deputy director came and patted my shoulder, saying that nothing would be delayed;
colleagues came and handed over file checklists, as if moving me from the sickbed back to the office;
my wife came, with the child, smiling as she said get well soon and we’ll go home together.
And none of them knew that what I feared most was that word they spoke in front of me—“home.”
Back where?
Back to that building I visited every day but never truly entered—my office?
Or back to that cage woven of human obligations and regulations—which I mistook for a career.

I began to loathe well-wishing. Only one person didn’t ask me “how’s progress,” only asked “where does it hurt.” Ah He, the cleaner. This man understood “procedure” less than any visitor, yet understood how to tuck a blanket when someone is about to break. He didn’t talk about dignity, nor about heavenly justice, and certainly not that word “persevere.” He only asked whether I was too warm, whether I was thirsty.

That night, for the first time, I wondered: have I spent my whole life arranging other people’s fates, yet dared not ask a single question—who arranges my fate?

Later, as my illness grew worse, I allowed myself to leave that sentence behind.
I thought of many things from the past, like scraps of paper falling from an old ledger:
the year I stood firm at a subordinate hearing and shut down a village committee’s tearful plea, because “there is a procedure”;
the year I refused to grant a small business an extension on its payment, because it “would set a precedent”;
the year I advised a colleague not to say “no” in a meeting, because it “would make the bureau look bad”;
the year I said on the telephone, “we’ll talk tomorrow,” and pushed a family’s hope ten days into the future.
At the time, I thought I was being efficient. In truth, I was only becoming more and more afraid to tell myself, “This is wrong.”

The hospital room was very cold. Not because the temperature was low, but because everyone tried to fit discomfort into the cheapest possible context. The doctor said “complicated,” the nurse said “under observation,” the family said “it’s nothing.” I heard myself answer inside my head: not a single word can cover my fear.

Someone came to report on a new project. He paused at the doorway, as if afraid I would hear. Still, he placed the papers by the bedside, his voice so light he might have been a passing stranger.
I looked at that paper, and my fingers ached faintly again: signature, instructions, confirmation. Every black mark meant someone could replace me.
Yes, the papers themselves were not evil; what was evil was that I had mistaken them for life itself.

At last, I understood a truth: many people don’t “turn bad” before doing wrong; they discover they’ve long since forfeited the right to do good only after doing wrong has become a habit.

I remembered when I was in my early twenties, I did a stretch of volunteering at Liangcheng North Gate Primary School, delivering coal to students’ homes. The hardest part then wasn’t the long road; it was seeing them more clearly than I saw myself: they, at least, were honest—so honest that each face was a real face.
Now, every face wears its duty; even my love for my wife wore the outer garment of duty—she didn’t need to hear my troubles, only that I showed up on time as stipulated. On the phone she would tell the child, “Daddy has to work late,” and the child would change “Daddy” into a single word: busy.

The pain was more pronounced deep in the night. I gazed at the ceiling as if searching for an exit in a vast, empty hall. Every breath felt borrowed. In that moment, I no longer protested anything; I only asked for one thing: that someone would admit for me that I was afraid.

That evening after dinner, Ah He sat on a chair, holding my old notebook. It was already filled with dates, milestones, numbers, explanations. The handwriting resembled mechanical gears.
He said, “Why don’t you let me delete a bit for you? Start by not writing yourself into clauses.”
I said, “Delete what?”
“All the proof you’ve filled in. Where you wrote that you’re not human.”
He laughed—when he truly laughed, there was a trace of old cigarette smoke in his gums, just like the dark stains left from his youth.

I was silent for a long time, then suddenly said, “Do you know the word ‘sin’?”
He nodded.
“I don’t want to think of myself as a bad man.”
“Then first, treat living as a thing—does a thing have a criminal charge?”
He wasn’t posing a rhetorical question; he was giving me space.

Later, I thought about it for a long time before I understood that his remark, which sounded like a joke, was a final absolution for me.

“The way you lived was, originally, good intention.”
“But when does good intention turn into harm?”
“When you offer yourself up to bear everything wrong in place of others.”

I didn’t sleep all night.
My thoughts jumped from “Who am I?” to “What have I done?” and then to “Can I still make amends?”
Make amends—like patching a crack: even with the biggest patch, it’s covered, but it’s not a repair.

The next day, several familiar people came, with eyes that didn’t dare to look.
They all said, “Get well soon,”
and I nodded to each.
But I knew that for the first time, I had been sick not in the illness but in the system for much longer. The system divides people into “usable” and “temporarily usable,” and I happened to be the latter. The temporarily usable are the most dangerous group, because they fear being discarded above all.

I asked my wife, “If I don’t come back, what will you do?”
She froze, as if thrown into a pool of water.
“Don’t say that.” She answered hastily, anger in her eyes first, then tears, and finally fear.
I didn’t ask again. Because I knew her answer didn’t need to be judged for truth.
She was answering with a lover’s instinct, not with what my soul needed.

I remembered once standing by the same kind of bedside, reassuring the family of a subordinate: “Just hold on a little longer.” I remembered that person, after hearing it, simply nodded and didn’t look at me.
That was the first time I had turned an empty phrase into a weapon, gently batting someone else’s reality back into the “manageable.”
From that moment on, I was already far from the truth.

The true threshold didn’t arrive when the pain was heaviest; it arrived the first time I admitted: I no longer wanted to be the person who “decides the correct course” on behalf of others.

I asked Ah He to take my notebook away. He turned back and looked at me for three seconds, then asked, “Want to write something?”
I said, “Write a letter.”
“To whom?”
“To everyone I thought was important.”
“You’re going to write it?”
I said, “I’ve never known how to write letters.”
“Then start with this line.”

I wrote the first line:

“I thought I was taking care of this city, but in fact I was only taking care of my reputation.”

By the thirtieth line I stopped, my hand aching. I almost deleted the second line:

“If you can remember me, please remember that I was once afraid.”

In this world, fear seems to have no place in the archives. Archives record only outcomes—a little less humanity, a little less night. But I wanted to write this down, at least on paper, at least for myself.

I deleted that line again. I didn’t dare. Not because I feared others’ judgment, but because it was only then that I realized: I had always been waiting for someone else to grant me permission to “be allowed to be afraid.”
Allowed?
If even fear requires approval, then how much freedom is left in living?

Later, I stopped writing those “important words.” I turned the page over and wrote names. The names of subordinates, my wife’s name, Ah He’s name. Each one I wrote was like dropping a pebble into a well, the sound growing softer and softer.

One early morning, a thought opened up in me.
Not a miracle, nor a flash of insight.
Just the sense that if I dragged on any longer, my heart would be scooped empty. I needed to set down, first of all, the hardest words of this whole life.
I told the attending physician I didn’t want any invasive procedures.
The doctor looked at me and was silent for three seconds.
“Have you thought it through?”
“I’ve thought it through.”
He said he respected my wishes.
Before that, I had never heard the word “respect” spoken so gently.

Outside the ward, the world went on.
Calls from colleagues came in plenty, meetings convened as usual, whose car rotated out, whose microphone took over. I had already exited this theater.
But exiting does not equal liberation. Exiting only removes you from the stage, while the audience still follows the lines you once spoke.

I heard Ah He downstairs exchanging a few jokes with a cleaner, his voice very soft. That voice reminded me of the man who sold hot soy milk on the street corner many years ago. Every rainy day he would lift the pot a little higher, because he knew which way the wind would blow.
Goodness in the world is often like that: not asking whether you are right, only asking whether you can hold on until the next moment.

That evening, my wife held my hand. For the first time, no advice, no command, no “hang in there.” Just holding.
“Are you afraid?” she asked.
“Yes.”
She said nothing more.
Suddenly, for the first time, I felt ashamed and relieved for all the years I had lived—because I had finally spoken the truth to myself.

I said:
“I haven’t lived for myself, nor for this world. I’ve lived like a stand-in.”
She said:
“Then whose place are you standing in now?”
“Yours.”
“Everyone is standing in someone’s place.” She smiled a little, the rims of her eyes red.
That smile was like a door not quite shut: wind came in, but the wind was like light.

After that, I had almost no expectations, only a few small requests:
If one day you have to make decisions again, please don’t look at the reports first, look at the people.
If one day you have to sign your name again, think first of the person asking, “Will someone be waiting with dinner tonight?”
If one day you must rush a deadline, think first that what you’re rushing is not a project, but a life.

I wrote these words on paper; the edges had water marks, though I couldn’t tell where they fell from.
I don’t dare call it a last testament, nor give it a name.
I only know that, for all those years in Liangcheng, what I was most skilled at was finding excuses for myself; the very last thing I am willing to do now is to make no more excuses for myself.

The night was very still, like a freshly washed glass through which you can see everything underneath.
When the pain returned, I no longer resisted it.
I thought of a day long ago when I sent a child’s letter of commendation back to its place because “the procedure was different”—that was the most shameful afternoon.
I also thought of Ah He that night changing my blanket, and how he casually pushed the medicine boxes on the table aside without mentioning price or treatment plan.
I thought of myself in the office, uttering that eternal slogan:
“As long as we follow the process, the problem can be solved.”
That sentence kept splintering in my mind.

Now I only want it to stop before my final moment.
I don’t want to be written down as a good man.
I want to be remembered as someone who, at last, understood.

Half an hour before dawn, it began to snow outside.
Snow fell on the eaves of Liangcheng; in some unknown room, a person might have been woken by the sound.
I felt my chest growing urgent again; Ah He, holding a blanket, sat down by the bed.
“Go on,” he said softly.
“Go where?”
“Toward your own side.”
Such awkward words, yet to my ears they sounded the most like a prayer.

I smiled very lightly.
“Didn’t you say to go a little lighter?”
He nodded. “That’s right. Start by going a little lighter.”

I thought of the first time in my life I didn’t rush a document, didn’t review a case, didn’t say “no.”
I thought of my son on the phone saying, “Daddy, I drew a tree today,”
and I said, “Show me through a magnifying glass,”
not knowing when that sentence had turned into a farewell in my heart.

The white light on the ceiling slowly dimmed.
Someone asked whether it hurt now.
I said:
“It’s bearable.”
The real answer was:
“At last, I’m beginning to go a little lighter.”
